# Prahari — experimental results

Generated by `scripts/run_experiments.py` on 12 simulated wards of 8 beds. Ground truth comes from the same physiology the twin uses; the twin only ever sees the degraded ward chart.

## 1. Hidden state from bedside signs

Can the twin place a patient in the right Stevenson quadrant — and estimate cardiac index and filling pressure, which no ward instrument measures — from what a nurse charts? Same patients in both rows; only the charting interval changes.

| Charting | Readings / patient | Quadrant | Wet vs dry | Cold vs warm | CI error | PCWP error |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hourly | 15.4 | 95.8% | 97.9% | 97.9% | 0.17 L/min/m² | 0.87 mmHg |
| 4-hourly (Indian step-down) | 4.4 | 83.3% | 90.6% | 92.7% | 0.28 L/min/m² | 1.60 mmHg |

n = 96 patient-states per row. The twin fits the whole chart jointly rather than trusting the last reading, and uses the congestion signs (JVP, oedema, weight) and perfusion sign (capillary refill) a ward already records. More frequent readings help — hourly charting reaches 95.8% — but a hand-written chart every four hours still places 83.3% of patients in the right profile. The twin works from the chart a ward already keeps, and improves with every monitor it can read.

## 2. Are the what-if answers right?

At each charting round every drug in the formulary is given twice from the same moment: to the twin (fitted only from the chart) and to the ground-truth patient. This is the validation the digital-twin literature identifies as usually missing: prediction accuracy says nothing about whether counterfactuals are correct.

| Measure | Result |
|---|---|
| Direction of drug effect correct | **98.9%** (3080 drug-effect comparisons) |
| Predicted vs true change in cardiac index | r = **0.97**, MAE 0.12 L/min/m² |
| Predicted vs true change in filling pressure | MAE 0.35 mmHg |
| Twin picks the best drug in hindsight | 69.0% of 255 decisions |

**Decision regret, measured on the real patient** (severity above the best choice in hindsight; lower is better):

| Policy | Mean regret |
|---|---|
| Prahari — twin chooses | **0.005** |
| Furosemide whenever NEWS2 ≥ 5 (usual ward reflex) | 0.047 |
| Wait and watch | 0.058 |
| IV furosemide for every patient | 0.153 |

In 129 of 255 decisions a reflex diuretic left the real patient worse than doing nothing — the compensated and cold-dry patients, where diuresis is clinically contraindicated. The twin's choice was harmful in 7 of those.

**Why a reflex diuretic goes wrong** — the same drug, by true haemodynamic profile:

| True profile | Decisions | Furosemide helps | Furosemide harms | Twin names the profile |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A — warm, dry | 70 | 0.0% | 100.0% | 84.3% |
| B — warm, wet | 89 | 71.9% | 28.1% | 85.4% |
| L — cold, dry | 34 | 0.0% | 100.0% | 91.2% |
| C — cold, wet | 62 | 79.0% | 0.0% | 91.9% |

Diuretics help wet patients and harm dry ones — the bedside teaching, reproduced. A cold-dry patient is hypotensive and tachycardic, so an early-warning score fires on them, and the reflex response for a heart-failure patient is the wrong one. Telling these apart is what the twin is for.

**Caveat.** These checks run against simulated patients whose drug responses follow the twin's own pharmacology, so they show the twin recovers the right patient state to answer what-ifs correctly — not that the drug effect sizes are right for real patients. Section 4 tests that on real ICU data, where they are not yet calibrated.

## 3. As an alarm: honestly, no better than NEWS2

Each ward replayed round by round; both systems see only what has been charted so far. A deterioration counts as caught if the system alarmed at or before the true onset of cold-wet.

| At default settings | Caught | Median warning | Alarms / patient-day | Precision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NEWS2 ≥ 5 | 93.1% (27/29) | 5.0 h | 3.24 | 58.5% |
| Prahari | 62.1% (18/29) | 3.875 h | 1.90 | 86.9% |

| ≥ 4 | 3.41 | 93.1% | 57.3% |   | 2 h | 1.58 | 37.9% | 93.7% |
| ≥ 5 | 3.24 | 93.1% | 58.5% |   | 4 h | 1.63 | 44.8% | 93.3% |
| ≥ 6 | 2.29 | 86.2% | 80.0% |   | 6 h | 1.73 | 51.7% | 90.2% |
| ≥ 7 | 1.94 | 79.3% | 91.3% |   | 8 h | 1.80 | 58.6% | 90.1% |
| ≥ 8 | 1.67 | 55.2% | 96.4% |   | 10 h | 1.88 | 65.5% | 88.4% |
| ≥ 9 | 1.50 | 34.5% | 98.0% |   | 12 h | 1.97 | 65.5% | 85.9% |
| | | | |   | 16 h | 2.10 | 69.0% | 82.0% |
| | | | |   | 20 h | 2.26 | 75.9% | 78.4% |

**Result.** At matched alarm burden NEWS2 catches as many deteriorations or more. The twin is not a better alarm, and we do not claim it is. Its misses are concentrated early in a record: with one or two readings a downward trend is not yet knowable. Prahari is therefore designed to sit **behind** the early-warning score — NEWS2 says who is sick; the twin says what kind of sick, what to do, and what happens if you wait.

## 4. Real patients: MIMIC-IV

MIMIC-IV Clinical Database Demo v2.2 (PhysioNet, ODbL): 32 heart-failure ICU stays from 22 patients, median age 71, 13 women, median stay 4.1 days, 1004 four-hourly charting rounds. The twin is fitted from the chart alone: heart rate, blood pressure, SpO₂ (only when the patient is on room air), respiratory rate (only when not ventilated), oedema grade and capillary refill. CVP, cardiac output and pulmonary-artery pressures are withheld and used only to score it.

### Hidden state against withheld measurements

| Twin estimate vs measured | Stays | Points (per stay) | Correlation r | Mean abs error | Bias |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Central venous pressure | 10 | 57 (21, 9, 9, 4, 4, 4, 3, 1, 1, 1) | 0.30 | 5.01 mmHg | -3.7 mmHg |
| Cardiac index | 3 | 27 (25, 1, 1) | 0.62 | 0.71 L/min/m² | 0.6 L/min/m² |
| Wedge pressure vs PA diastolic | 3 | 9 (4, 4, 1) | 0.53 | 4.24 mmHg | -3.0 mmHg |

Of 2 measured cardiac indices below 2.2 (cold), the twin called 0 cold; it called 2 of 25 warm readings cold.

**Reading.** The estimates move with the measurements, but calibration is off in one consistent direction: on real ICU patients the twin reads output too high and filling pressure too low — it sees them as less sick than they are. That is the unsafe direction, and it is the first thing to fix with a larger cohort. Part of the pressure bias is expected physiology: PA diastolic pressure normally runs a few mmHg above wedge pressure, and the model's JVP-to-filling mapping was never calibrated on real data. The sample is too small to recalibrate on without fitting the answer key, so we have not.

### Forecasting the next round

909 forecasts: fitted on the chart so far, predicting the next round four hours ahead. In 140 of them a drug was started in between, and the twin was told which.

| Mean abs error | Twin | Twin, drugs ignored | Persistence (no change) | Twin closer than persistence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Heart rate (bpm) | 9.5 | 9.1 | 8.2 | 41% |
| Systolic (mmHg) | 15.2 | 14.8 | 12.6 | 42% |
| Diastolic (mmHg) | 9.4 | 9.2 | 9.5 | 51% |
| Mean arterial (mmHg) | 10.2 | 9.9 | 9.3 | 47% |

When mean arterial pressure actually moved by 5 mmHg or more (562 rounds), the twin predicted the direction 59.8% of the time.

**Reading.** Over four hours, "nothing changes" is hard to beat on real ICU patients, and the twin does not beat it on average: these patients are titrated continuously by staff, and the twin knows only which drugs were started, not dose changes. Forecasting raw vitals is not what the twin is for, but we report it because a twin whose forward model loses to persistence should say so.

Telling the twin which drugs were started did not improve its forecast (mean-arterial error 10.2 with the drugs, 9.9 without). The drug effect sizes are set from pharmacology for a proof of concept and are not calibrated to real ICU dosing. The counterfactual results in §2 are therefore validated against the simulator's pharmacology, not yet against real patients' responses.

### Did the twin see what the clinicians acted on?

Twin profile at the last charted round before a treatment was started, against all rounds. Clinicians' choices are not ground truth, but a twin that reads patients sensibly should lean the same way.

| At the round before… | Starts | Twin says wet | Twin says cold |
|---|---|---|---|
| any round (base rate) | 972 | 57.6% | 4.9% |
| a loop diuretic | 82 | 57.3% | 7.3% |
| an inotrope | 6 | 83.3% | 33.3% |
| a vasopressor | 44 | 45.5% | 11.4% |

**Reading.** Before an inotrope the twin read patients as cold 6.8× as often as at an average round (6 starts); before a vasopressor the twin read patients as cold 2.3× as often as at an average round (44 starts); before a loop diuretic it read them as wet no more often than usual (57.3% vs 57.6%, 82 starts) — no signal. The counts for inotropes are too small to rely on.


## 5. The twin at home

The ward-fitted heart is sent home. Over days the question is fluid: a patient retaining a few hundred millilitres a day slides into decompensation over one to three weeks. The same physics, run at its daily steady state, says what that does to weight, resting heart rate, HRV, time awake in bed, sleep and steps; the twin reads those back from a band and a scale and forecasts the day this particular heart tips over.

**Simulated cohort.** 96 discharged patients from the simulated wards, each sent home twice — once stable, once retaining 150–400 mL/day from a random day — 30 days each. The truth runs on the patient's true heart; the twin only gets the heart the ward fitted. Day-to-day noise and missing days are borrowed from the real Fitbit volunteers (resting heart rate, steps, sleep, awake-in-bed time); HRV and weight are simulated. A false alarm is a flag on a stable patient's day; a catch is a flag after retention starts and before the decompensation day.

*Band and bathroom scale*

| Detector | False alarms (stable patient-days) | Stable patients ever flagged | Decompensations caught in advance | Median days ahead |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Weight rule: +2 kg in 3 days | 0.0% | 0 of 96 | 17 of 91 | 4.0 |
| Weight rule: +2 kg over first week | 0.5% | 4 of 96 | 84 of 91 | 4.5 |
| Drift score (previous) | 1.4% | 17 of 96 | 35 of 91 | 3.0 |
| Home twin | 0.5% | 9 of 96 | 91 of 91 | 6.0 |

*Band only*

| Detector | False alarms (stable patient-days) | Stable patients ever flagged | Decompensations caught in advance | Median days ahead |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Drift score (previous) | 0.8% | 13 of 96 | 7 of 91 | 3.0 |
| Home twin | 5.2% | 30 of 96 | 61 of 91 | 4.0 |

The home twin at other alarm thresholds (0.5 was fixed in advance):

| Threshold | Band + scale: false alarms | caught | days ahead | Band only: false alarms | caught | days ahead |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.3 | 1.4% | 100.0% | 6.0 | 11.1% | 74.7% | 6.0 |
| 0.5 | 0.5% | 100.0% | 6.0 | 5.2% | 67.0% | 4.0 |
| 0.7 | 0.0% | 98.9% | 5.0 | 2.9% | 56.0% | 3.0 |
| 0.9 | 0.0% | 93.4% | 3.0 | 0.8% | 42.9% | 3.0 |

**This heart's limit.** Across the cohort the true decompensation point runs from 2.2 to 3.8 litres retained — weak hearts go home fuller and tip over sooner. From the ward fit alone the twin places it within a median 175 mL, biased -164 mL (early, the safe side).

**At the twin's first alarm** (91 patients, band and scale): the forecast day of decompensation was off by 2.2 days on average, and the actual day fell inside the twin's 10–90% range for 89.0%. Asked whether each plan keeps the patient out of decompensation for 14 days, it agreed with the truth for no change 97.8%, fluid restriction 63.7% and a doubled diuretic 86.8%. Median days a fluid restriction buys: 3.7 true, 5.5 by the twin. The plan effects are simulated pharmacology checked against the same simulator — the same caveat as the ward what-ifs.

**Real volunteers.** 11 Fitbit volunteers (Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.53894, CC BY 4.0), each record written to an Apple Health `export.xml` and read back (11/11 intact), then read by each of 8 ward-fitted hearts. They are healthy, so any flag on their unmodified records is a false alarm. The generic decompensation overlay (from day 12; written before the home twin, so it does not follow its physiology) tests detection. The scale weight and HRV are simulated.

| Setting | Detector | False alarms (real user-days) | Overlay caught | Median days to catch |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Band + scale | Weight rule: +2 kg in 3 days | 0.0% of 1784 | 0 of 48 | — |
| Band + scale | Weight rule: +2 kg over first week | 0.0% of 1784 | 48 of 48 | 12.0 |
| Band + scale | Drift score (previous) | 2.0% of 1784 | 38 of 48 | 10.0 |
| Band + scale | Home twin | 0.6% of 1784 | 47 of 48 | 9.0 |
| Band only | Drift score (previous) | 1.2% of 1784 | 32 of 48 | 12.5 |
| Band only | Home twin | 3.9% of 1784 | 27 of 48 | 12.0 |

**Reading.** With a scale, the twin caught 91 of 91 a median 6.0 days ahead, against 17 for the '2 kg in 3 days' rule — a slow gain never trips it — and 84 (4.5 days ahead) for '2 kg over the first week'. That is a modest edge over the best weight rule, not a different league: here every decompensation is fluid accumulation, so weight is close to a perfect signal. In real patients it rises before only some admissions. Without a scale the twin still caught 61 of 91 (4.0 days ahead) where the previous drift score caught 7, but at 5.2% false alarms on stable days. What the twin adds that no rule can is the forecast itself — how many days, for this heart — and what a diuretic change or fluid restriction would buy.

On real volunteers' records the twin false-alarmed on 0.6% of days with a scale and 3.9% on the band alone, against 1.2% for the previous drift score on the band alone. The band-only alarm is not yet good enough to use by itself; with a scale it is.

## Limitations

- Sections 1–3 use simulated patients whose ground truth comes from the same physiology the twin uses; those numbers are an upper bound on real performance.
- The real-patient checks (§4) cover 32 ICU stays, and the measurements that test hidden state exist in few of them. They show direction and calibration, not clinical validity.
- The real wear data (§5) comes from healthy volunteers; the decompensation it is tested against is simulated, and so are HRV and the scale weight. In the simulated cohort every decompensation is fluid build-up, which flatters weight-based detection.
- The home twin treats day-to-day variation as independent. Real weight and activity also wander slowly, and the twin reads that wander as slow retention: on some healthy volunteers it reports fluid building at a few tens of mL a day. A random-walk term in its noise model is the next fix; it was not made after seeing these results.
- Home plan effects (fluid restriction, doubled diuretic) are first-order changes to net retention, checked only against the same simulator.
- No clinical validity is claimed. The next step is the full, credentialed MIMIC-IV database and a silent shadow run on a ward.
